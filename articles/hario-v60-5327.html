The V60 Is Not a Coffee Maker. It Is a Morning.

I bought a Hario V60 because it looked like something I should own. I keep using it because the ritual has become the point.

I bought a Hario V60 because everyone told me I should. It is a piece of clear glass shaped like a cone. It costs twenty dollars. It does nothing by itself. It does not heat water. It does not grind beans. It does not even hold coffee on its own—you need a paper filter, which you must buy separately, and which you must fold just so, with a crisp fold along the seam, or the water will bypass the coffee grounds and you will have weak, sad coffee.

I bought it because everyone told me to. The coffee blogs, the Instagram accounts, the YouTube channels—all of them swore by the V60. It is the pour-over dripper. It is the standard. If you care about coffee, you own a V60. I bought one because I wanted to be the kind of person who owns a V60.

"I bought a V60 because everyone said I should. I used it once and put it in a cupboard. Then I tried again. And again. Now it is the only way I make coffee."

by u/CoffeeSnob22 on r/Coffee

The first time I used it, I made a terrible cup. The water went too fast. The coffee was weak and sour. I poured the water too fast, or maybe too slow. I used too much coffee. Too little. The water was too hot. Too cold. Everything was wrong. I poured the cup down the sink and put the V60 in the cupboard.

I tried again the next morning. I weighed the coffee. I timed the pour. I measured the water temperature. I watched the bloom—the way the coffee grounds bloom and release gas when hot water first hits them, like a slow bloom of flower petals. I poured in circles, not too fast, not too slow. I waited for the drawdown. The coffee tasted like dirt. I poured it down the sink.

I tried again the next morning. And the next. And the next. Each time I changed one thing. I used less coffee. I used hotter water. I poured slower. I poured faster. I used a different filter. I ground the coffee finer. I ground it coarser. I kept notes in a notebook. I tracked variables like a scientist. And slowly, imperceptibly, the coffee got better.

"The V60 is not a coffee maker. It is a practice. You do not buy it to make coffee. You buy it to learn how to make coffee."

I have owned espresso machines. I have owned pour-over cones, French presses, pour-over drippers of every shape and size. The V60 is the one I reach for every morning. Not because it makes the best coffee—I am not sure it does. But because it makes me slow down. Because it forces me to pay attention. Because in a world that moves too fast, it forces me to slow down and pay attention to one thing: making coffee.
