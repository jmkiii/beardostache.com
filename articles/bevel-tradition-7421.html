The Safety Razor Is Not a Fashion Statement

I bought a safety razor because I was tired of paying five dollars for a cartridge that dulled in a week. I kept using it because shaving stopped feeling like a chore.

I bought my first safety razor because I was angry. I was angry at the cost of cartridge razors—four dollars a cartridge, three cartridges in a pack, twelve dollars for a week of shaves. I was angry at the marketing, at the commercials with smiling models gliding razors across already-smooth skin, at the promise of a "close, comfortable shave" that never lasted more than three days. I bought a safety razor because I was angry, and I kept using it because I liked the ritual.

The safety razor is not complicated. It is a handle and a blade. That is it. There is no pivoting head, no lubrication strip, no pivoting head that "adapts to the contours of your face." There is a handle and a blade, and you hold it at a thirty-degree angle and you shave with the grain, and then you shave across the grain, and then you shave against the grain, and you are done. It takes three minutes. It leaves your face smooth. It costs twenty-five cents per blade.

The modern razor is designed to be replaced. The handle is plastic, the cartridge is plastic, the blade is encased in plastic so you never touch the blade itself. You do not touch the blade because the manufacturer does not want you to touch the blade—if you touched it, you would realize it costs fifty cents to make, and you would not pay five dollars for it. The cartridge is designed to dull quickly, to dull after three or four shaves, so that you buy another one. The entire system is designed to make you buy more cartridges.

The safety razor is different. The blade is exposed. You can see it. You can feel it. You know exactly how sharp it is because you can see it. You change the blade when it dulls, which is every week or two depending on how coarse your beard is. A blade costs twenty-five cents. A cartridge costs four dollars. Over a year, the difference is significant. Over a lifetime, it is thousands of dollars.

The 4080 writes about specifications. He writes about blade sharpness and blade steel and edge retention. I write about what it feels like to stand in front of the mirror every morning and perform the same small ritual, day after day, year after year.

I have owned electric razors. I have owned cartridge razors. I have owned cheap disposable razors. I keep coming back to the safety razor—not because it is the best shave I have ever had, but because it is the one I trust. It does not pretend to be anything other than what it is. It is a blade on a handle. It cuts hair. It does its job.
